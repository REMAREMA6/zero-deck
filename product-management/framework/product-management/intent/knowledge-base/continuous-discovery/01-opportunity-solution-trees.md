## What is an opportunity solution tree?

An opportunity solution tree is a simple way of visually representing the paths you might take to reach a desired outcome.

- The root of the tree is your **desired outcome** — the business need that reflects how your team can create business value.
- Next is the **opportunity space** — the customer needs, pain points, and desires that, if addressed, will drive your desired outcome.
- Below the opportunity space is the **solution space** — the solutions you are exploring.
- Below the solution space are **assumption tests** — how you evaluate which solutions create customer value in a way that drives business value.

## Benefits

Opportunity solution trees help product trios:
1. Resolve the tension between business needs and customer needs
2. Build and maintain a shared understanding of how they might reach their desired outcome
3. Adopt a continuous mindset
4. Unlock better decision-making
5. Unlock faster learning cycles
6. Build confidence in knowing what to do next
7. Unlock simpler stakeholder management

## Who should create one

Designed to help a product trio (PM + designer + engineer) chart the best path to their desired outcome — a team activity. Anyone can create one for practice; externalizes and visualizes thinking.

## Prerequisites (don't skip)

1. A theory of your target customer and the value proposition you plan to offer them.
2. A clearly defined outcome.
3. Three to four story-based customer interviews conducted before your first tree. "Opportunity solution trees are nearly impossible to create when you don't have good inputs."

## How to create one (9 steps)

1. Meet the prerequisites.
2. Put your outcome at the top of the tree.
3. Map the opportunity space (messy process of synthesis).
4. Choose a target opportunity.
5. Brainstorm solutions for the target opportunity only (not across the whole tree).
6. Choose three solutions to explore further.
7. Break the solutions into underlying assumptions; prioritize which to test first.
8. Test the riskiest assumptions from each of the three ideas.
9. As data comes in, evaluate and decide: throw ideas away and generate new ones; decide one is good enough and refine for delivery; or decide you can't deliver on the opportunity and choose a new target opportunity.

## Understanding the outcome at the top

- **Three types of outcomes** (from Continuous Discovery Habits): business outcomes (health of the business; financial metrics), product outcomes (customer behavior or sentiment in the product), traction metrics (adoption of a single feature).
- **Product outcomes are the right scope for discovery.** Business outcomes are hard for a team to know how to impact; traction metrics are too narrow.
- **The outcome focuses on business value first** because when we create business value we earn the right to serve the customer over time. The opportunity space then ensures we reach the outcome in a customer-centric way. This resolves the tension between business and customer value. Cautionary examples: Google Reader (beloved, shut down), Dark Sky (beloved, shuttered by Apple). "Opportunity solution trees help us discover products that are both desirable now and viable over time."
- **Multiple outcomes:** prioritize them yourself and build a tree for the most important one — you can't develop a rich understanding of an opportunity space for multiple outcomes at once.
- **Multiple teams on one outcome:** split the outcome so each team has its own — by customer segment (e.g., tenure) or by customer journey step (onboarding, activation, retention). One unique outcome per team.

## Understanding the opportunity space

- **Opportunity** = an unmet customer need, pain point, or desire. Opportunities expose gaps in the market where you can immediately create value.
- **Opportunity vs solution:** a solution is a product, service, or feature offered to address an opportunity. Example opportunities: "I'm hungry", "I don't know what to eat for dinner", "I don't have time to cook dinner." Solutions: order takeout, go out to eat, subscribe to a meal prep service.
- **Test that an opportunity is not a solution in disguise:** ask "Is there more than one way to address this opportunity?" "I want to go out to eat" is a solution (only one way to address it). Reframe with "Why?" → "I don't have time to cook", "I want to eat right now", "I want something that tastes better than what I know how to make."
- **Why "opportunity" not "problem":** we don't just solve problems; products also address desires (Disneyland, ice cream, mountain biking). Opportunities include needs, pain points, and desires.
- **How to find opportunities:** story-based interviews — "Tell me about a time when…". Opportunities emerge from stories, with rich context (when it occurred, what the customer was trying to do).
- **Don't generate opportunities from what you already know** — you bring biases and half-truths; limiting the tree to interview-derived opportunities guarantees you work on real needs.
- **Other sources (sales calls, support tickets, feature requests, analytics)** usually lack context; use them as inspiration for upcoming interviews, not as tree inputs.

## Mapping the opportunity space

- **When ready:** start after three to four customer stories — enough to prevent overreacting to a single story, not so many you get analysis paralysis. Interview continuously (week over week); for every three to four interviews, revise the opportunity space.
- **Structuring (4 steps):** (1) create an experience map of all collected stories; (2) map the moments in the experience map to top-level opportunities; (3) group each individual opportunity under the top-level moment it occurred in; (4) structure each branch one at a time, creating parent-child and sibling relationships.
- **Messiness over time:** normal at first; as you interview more the structure stabilizes — top-level key moments shouldn't change often; refinements come from exploring branches downward.
- **Customer segments:** add segments at the top only when the team serves multiple audiences (marketplace sides, complex ecosystems) and the tree should match the team's remit.

## Choosing a target opportunity

- Work on **one small opportunity at a time** (kanban's limited WIP); enables iterative value delivery and compare-and-contrast of multiple solutions.
- **Choose as soon as you have a first draft** of the opportunity space. "Embrace a 'crummy first draft' mindset." Push to solution exploration and assumption testing ASAP — that's where most discovery learning happens.
- **Assess and prioritize opportunities** with four factor sets: (1) opportunity sizing — how many customers affected and how often; (2) market factors — effect on product's market position; (3) company factors — support of mission, vision, strategic objectives; (4) customer factors — importance to customers and satisfaction with existing solutions.
- **Don't include effort when assessing opportunities** — effort belongs to solution evaluation; an opportunity assumed too hard may have a dead-simple solution.

## Understanding solutions

- **Solution** = product, feature, service, workflow, process, documentation, or anything offered to address a known opportunity.
- **One solution per opportunity** unlocks a continuous cadence; project-sized solutions that address multiple opportunities slow you down.
- **Consider multiple solutions** (≥3) for the target opportunity — compare-and-contrast improves decisions. Compare when there's risk, when the opportunity is a differentiator, or when you need an innovative solution.
- **Decide via assumption testing** — testing the assumptions ideas depend on gives the data needed to decide.
- **When ready to build:** depends on how much risk the solution has, how much risk the organization can bear, and how much time you have. Putting a solution in the delivery backlog is a bet; discovery reduces the risk of that bet. When new to discovery, do a little more discovery than you think you need.

## Tools, scope and iterations

- Any visual tool with shapes and arrows works (Miro, FigJam, Mural, Lucidchart); templates exist for Miro/Mural/FigJam.
- **Start a new tree** when the directional component of the outcome changes (e.g., switching to acquisition or changing the aha moment), not when the target number changes.
- **Update frequency:** revisit the opportunity space every three to four customer interviews (roughly every three to four weeks at weekly interviewing).
- **Company-wide tree:** not recommended — the tree is for one product trio charting one desired outcome; use KPI trees or company-wide experience maps for strategy/vision.

## Stakeholders, roadmaps, vision

- **Sharing:** answer who needs to be kept up to date and at what level of detail; match level to stakeholder (full tree for close stakeholders, highlights for others).
- **Roadmap connection:** with date-based roadmaps, the roadmap lists solutions already matched to opportunities and vetted by assumption tests. With outcome-oriented roadmaps (e.g., Now-Next-Future), the roadmap may include opportunities and solutions from the tree plus outcomes to explore next.
- **Vision connection:** product vision tells where we are headed; opportunity solution trees help get there. Visions are high-level, rarely including per-team outcomes, opportunities, or solutions.
