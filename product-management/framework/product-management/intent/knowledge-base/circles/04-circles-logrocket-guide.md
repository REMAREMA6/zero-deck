# The CIRCLES method: A PM's guide to talking about design

**Source:** LogRocket Blog (third-party breakdown, supplementary)
**URL:** https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/circles-method-pm-guide-talking-about-design/
**Author:** Surbhi Sooni (product leader and strategist), Feb 10, 2023
**Fetched:** 2026-09-30

A product manager's responsibility is to identify the needs of their product's users while also creating and improving the features that meets those needs. In doing so, PMs may sometimes rush straight to the solutions without fully understanding the problems these solutions will resolve. This can sometimes lead to needs that are half-met or solutions that are only partially solved.

Lewis C. Lin, an author and entrepreneur, created the CIRCLES method framework as a guideline to give complete and thoughtful responses to any design question. It is a good way to slow down the process and save PMs from jumping straight to the solution.

## What does CIRCLES stand for?

CIRCLES is an acronym that describes seven steps to solve a design problem. These linear steps are:

1. Comprehend the situation
2. Identify the customer
3. Report the customer's needs
4. Cut through prioritization
5. List solutions
6. Evaluate trade-offs
7. Summarize the recommendation

### 1. Comprehend the situation

This is the first step towards solving the design problem. The CIRCLES method suggests you should clarify as much information as possible. You can ask the basic who, what, where, why and how questions. For example: What is it? Who is it for? Where does it happen? Why do they need it? How does it work? If the interviewer can't clarify these questions, you should make assumptions based on what you know and convey those assumptions to the interviewer.

### 2. Identify the customer

The CIRCLES method guidelines focus on a single customer segment during the interview that lets you propose an amazing product. A 2x2 matrix is a powerful way to visualize your persona that lists the archetypes' behavior, demography, needs, goals, and frustration.

### 3. Report the customer's needs

The candidate describes the customer's need, user requirements, or use cases. Typically presented in a popular format called user story: "As a [ROLE], I want [GOAL/DESIRE] so that [OUTCOME/BENEFITS]." The user story doesn't describe how the solution works.

### 4. Cut through prioritization

The backlog item that a PM prioritizes based on value, time, and effort. Picking one helps narrow down the choices and showcase the ability to prioritize and make decisions. The prioritization matrix is a better way to articulate and think through all the criteria.

### 5. List solutions

Brainstorming the solutions. A PM comes up with as many ideas as possible, and can narrow them down based on feasibility, customer, and revenue criteria. This is the stage where one gets the opportunity to raise creative solutions and showcase lateral and innovative thinking.

### 6. Evaluate trade-offs

The PM critiques their solution based on the trade-offs and risks. Unless the trade-off is unbiased, an impressive solution might come up. Finding the right solution depends on all listed solutions' short- and long-term pros and cons.

### 7. Summarize the recommendation

The final step when a PM summarizes the final recommendation: clear, concise, eager to hear why the recommended product or feature benefits the user, citing reasons for preferring the solutions over others.

## How does the CIRCLES framework apply to product management?

PMs need to keep focus on the persona and communicate "why" they are building it. The CIRCLES method helps PMs prioritize features, feedback, experiments, and overall roadmap. It also allows PMs to ask the right questions during discovery to understand the actual pain points before jumping on the solutions. This approach helps PMs use lateral thinking rather than linear thinking.

## Alternative methods

- **Double Diamond** (British Design Council, 2005): Discover, Design, Develop, Deliver. Converging and diverging thinking. Preferable in workshop mode by designers; facilitator focused.
- **Lean Circle**: reduced version of CIRCLES: (1) Explore the problem, (2) Define, prioritize and test the options, (3) Deliver a solution. Used when time is a constraint.

## Benefits of the CIRCLES framework

- Allows for effective brainstorming
- Identifies the best solutions (when budget/effort hinders design sprints or Double Diamond workshops)
- Provides a structured answer

## Weaknesses of the CIRCLES framework

- Based on a few clarifying questions; the list of solutions comes up based on those questions and assumption of personas, and may be biased in prioritization.
- Narrows the scope of testing and validating the solution using actual prototypes; solutions are evaluated based on trade-offs of pre-conceived criteria (customer experience, feasibility, revenue).
