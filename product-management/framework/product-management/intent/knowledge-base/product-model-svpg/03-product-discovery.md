# Product Discovery

**Source:** Silicon Valley Product Group (Marty Cagan) — original article
**URL:** https://www.svpg.com/product-discovery/
**Author:** Marty Cagan, September 24, 2007
**Fetched:** 2026-09-30

Note: This is the 2007 article where Cagan went "all in" on the term product discovery (see also "The Origin of Product Discovery", 2020).

## The typical (broken) situation

A company gets excited about a product idea, and the product manager is asked to define it. Engineers will be free in 4 weeks, so the PM has 4 weeks. The PM plans best practices: opportunity assessment, user interviews, preliminary requirements, prototype with an interaction designer in week 3, user testing, then use cases and spec review with engineering.

But what happens isn't usually so great. During initial user discussions users aren't as excited as management was, or the PM struggles to come up with a prototype users can figure out, or users aren't excited about the ideas when they test it. But the time is up, the engineers are ready, and for the next three to six months engineering proceeds to build that same unusable and unexciting product, ships, and management is disappointed. Reliability isn't the problem, so engineering isn't to blame — they just built what they were asked to. Everyone knows it's the product manager's fault.

It doesn't help to talk to users, create prototypes, and test with users, if you don't adjust your course based on what you learn.

## Why "product discovery" instead of "requirements and design"

The notion of requirements and design as a sequential, predictable and scheduled phase is so ingrained that it's one of the most difficult habits for product organizations to break. Product organizations need to come to terms with the fact that the product invention process is fundamentally a creative process. It is more art than science.

"Product discovery" emphasizes two all-important points:

1. You need to discover whether there are real users out there that want this product — identify your market and validate the opportunity with your customers.
2. You need to discover a product solution to this problem that is usable, useful, and feasible — design your product and validate it with your customers and your engineering team.

Sometimes discovery is straightforward; other times extremely difficult. It's not so hard to discover and validate the market opportunity, but often it's very challenging to discover the solution, even with great designers and engineers — some problems are just truly hard (at least many of the ones worth pursuing).

## The pharmaceutical analogy

Drug companies go into the "discovery" phase fully aware there's no guarantee they'll come up with anything, or how long it may take. As an industry they have to come to terms with this uncertainty (and the risk is priced into their products). Yet with software, even though we all know it is very hard and the majority of software releases fail to meet their objectives, we still insist on scheduling the discovery phase like we're planning the construction of a house.

## Why management struggles with discovery

1. The discovery process just isn't predictable. Management fears you may spend months and end up with nothing to show for it. At least if they go ahead and build they can say they shipped something (the same reason many managers are uncomfortable with Scrum — they want to know how many 30-day sprints before done).
2. The perceived constrained resource in just about every software product organization is the engineers, and the thought of an engineering team sitting around doing nothing drives management nuts.

Ironically, it is precisely this reasoning that leads directly to wasted engineering resources. Almost every company does this discovery process by using the full engineering team for full release cycles to build software that is then QA'ed and deployed into production. That's why it typically takes so many companies three or more releases over one to two years to get something usable and useful — they are using the engineering organization to build a very, very expensive prototype, using live customers as unwitting test subjects. This is also why so many startups fail — most simply don't have the funding to go two years before they gain traction.

## The faster path

Get them to focus on the much faster product discovery process: once the solution that works is discovered — one that is usable, useful and feasible — then it's all about the engineering. Until that point, they don't need to hire so many engineers right away; the engineers they have can and should actively participate in product discovery; and to a degree the engineers can prepare the infrastructure while discovery is going on.

PMs can help management understand: consistently emphasize your obligation to ensure that what engineering builds must be useful and usable, and enlist their efforts to discover a successful solution — you'll start to move their focus to this most critical stage of the product development process.
