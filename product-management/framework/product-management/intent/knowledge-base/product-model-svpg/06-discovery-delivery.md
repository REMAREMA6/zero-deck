# Discovery – Delivery

**Source:** Silicon Valley Product Group (Marty Cagan) — original article
**URL:** https://www.svpg.com/discovery-delivery/
**Author:** Marty Cagan, October 30, 2020
**Fetched:** 2026-09-30

## The anti-pattern: splitting discovery and delivery into two teams

A damaging anti-pattern can arise where the product team devolves into two different teams — one doing product discovery ("discovery team") and another doing product delivery ("delivery team").

This is not how you want to work: there is a single, cross-functional product team, responsible for both discovery and delivery. This is key for both empowerment and innovation. Normally, each product team performs these two core activities continuously. At any given point different people may be focused on discovery or delivery activities (the product manager and product designer spend most of their time on discovery; engineers most of their time on delivery), but if you care about empowerment and innovation, each member of the product team should be engaged in both. It doesn't take much time for engineers to stay engaged with the ongoing discovery work.

## Common manifestations of the problem

1. The product manager believes it's her job to define the product, then hand off "the requirements" to the designer and engineers to implement. Pretty much all pure delivery teams work this way, and most feature teams do too (it may also be someone further upstream — a stakeholder or business owner — defining the solutions).
2. Especially egregious: the company outsources engineering to an agency or contractors.

Fundamentally, avoid having one person or group obtain the learnings, and then "hand off" what they learned for another group to execute on. The receiving group inevitably feels like mercenaries, and feels their contributions on better potential solutions would not be welcomed. It's the same reason corporate innovation labs have such a poor record of delivering results.

Even if the people making the decisions are very strong (a company founder or experienced head of product), there will still be a very high price paid in motivation and lack of ownership by the people on the receiving end, not to mention the substantially reduced likelihood of innovation. Over time the receiving-end people self-select to those willing to work like this — that's when founders and product leaders complain about the lack of skill, initiative or ownership from the people on their teams.

## The rule

Each cross-functional product team understands that they are responsible for both discovery and delivery work, and responsible for coming up with effective solutions and achieving the necessary results.
