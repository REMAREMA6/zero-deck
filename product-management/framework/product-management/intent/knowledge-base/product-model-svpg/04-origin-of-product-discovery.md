# The Origin of Product Discovery

**Source:** Silicon Valley Product Group (Marty Cagan) — original article
**URL:** https://www.svpg.com/the-origin-of-product-discovery/
**Author:** Marty Cagan, June 1, 2020
**Fetched:** 2026-09-30

Note: historical article on the origin of the term "product discovery".

## Two essential problems in software

We have always had, and likely always will have, two essential problems in software: we need to figure out the right product, and then we have to build the product right. Both matter, but the first is harder and that's where most innovation happens.

Starting around 2005 Cagan started trying out the term "discovery" for how we figure out what to build, and in 2007 he went all in on the term. Since then: first problem = discovery, second = delivery.

## Why "discovery" and not "requirements"

In 2007, Cagan was working on the first edition of INSPIRED with a publication deadline. Back then, just about every product manager talked about "gathering and defining requirements," but he considered this one of the fundamental reasons for so many failed products — talking about defining requirements was not only misguided, but arrogant.

He could see that in good teams, products were conceived by true collaborations between engineers, designers and product managers — not by some product manager declaring "these are the requirements, now go and design and build this."

He wanted a term that conjured up a very different image in the minds of product managers and product teams: an open mind; know what you can't know; admit what you don't know. Great products are the result of product, design and engineering working together to achieve an outcome, rather than pushing requirements down the chain and shipping output. Think in terms of "figuring out a solution to a problem we've been asked to solve" rather than "dictating the requirements of a feature we've been asked to build."

## Why the pharmaceutical industry

He found the term "discovery" from the pharmaceutical industry, the process they use to come up with a viable medication. Unlike software, the pharmaceutical industry acknowledged the risk up front — they expected many if not most of their medications would not end up being safe and effective enough to manufacture, distribute and sell.

## Why the term stuck and spread

He also liked "discovery" because it was technique agnostic — any number of techniques help with discovery and new ones emerge all the time: an MVP is a discovery technique, as is design thinking, customer development, and "Jobs To Be Done." This was important because he has seen so many techniques and methods come and go, so he didn't want to explicitly associate with any of them. He also liked that "discovery" encouraged product teams to think about the risks of value, usability, feasibility and viability.

He is generally reluctant to introduce new nomenclature, but today he is happy to see so many product teams understand the concept. His focus now is on making sure product teams work in an environment where they are empowered to do product discovery, rather than just given roadmaps to build.
