# Empowered Product Teams

**Source:** Silicon Valley Product Group (Marty Cagan) — original article
**URL:** https://www.svpg.com/empowered-product-teams/
**Author:** Marty Cagan, October 31, 2018
**Fetched:** 2026-09-30

Note: long narrative article (a talk Cagan gives at conferences, product meetups and corporate leadership teams). Central companion to "Empowered Product Teams" chapter area of the product model.

## The problem: teams not allowed to work as they need to

Many teams get quite good at the techniques of product discovery, yet are not able to actually apply those techniques and work the way they need to. Teaching a team how great teams operate is just half the problem, and it's the easier half. The issue is that they are often not allowed to work as they need to — they are not truly empowered.

Culture is important but not the full answer: Amazon, Google, Apple and Netflix are all very strong product companies, yet each has very different cultures. Something more fundamental and more specific — their views on their people, and how they should work together to solve problems.

## Extraordinary teams exist to serve customers, not "the business"

In most companies, technology teams exist "to serve the business." In strong product organizations, teams exist "to serve the customers, in ways that meet the needs of the business." That difference impacts nearly everything about the team and how it works, resulting in higher motivation and morale, and most importantly, higher levels of consistent innovation.

## Why leaders don't empower: trust

When asked why they don't empower teams, leaders' answer boils down to one word: trust. They don't trust the teams — they don't believe they have the people needed to truly empower them, so they direct the teams explicitly (the "command and control" model). When asked why they don't hire people they do trust, they argue they can't find, afford or attract the level of people that Google, Amazon, Apple and Netflix hire. Cagan's response: the vast majority of members of those companies' product teams are actually quite ordinary — the difference lies elsewhere: strong companies have different views on how to leverage their talent so ordinary people reach their true potential and achieve, together, extraordinary results.

## Leadership vs. Management

Moving to truly empowered teams does require moving away from command and control, but it does not mean fewer leaders and managers — it means stronger ones. Command and control is easier for the manager but leads directly to teams of mercenaries, and no real empowerment.

### The role of leadership — five major responsibilities

1. **Product Vision** — the shared objective for the product organization ("north star"). Gives the organization its purpose; attracts "missionaries" — people excited about and dedicated to the vision.
2. **Product Strategy** — the plan for accomplishing the product vision. Not a detailed schedule (though it may include major milestones); compelling and well-reasoned. Without it: teams struggle to see the path from today to the 5–10 year vision, and try to please too many types of customers in a single product, pleasing no one.
3. **Product Principles** — the nature of the products the organization believes it needs to produce; reflect values and strategic decisions that help teams decide under trade-offs.
4. **Product Priorities** — the high-level prioritization decision stating what's most important (e.g., via OKRs, where leaders define objectives and key results).
5. **Product Evangelism** — communicating the vision to the product organization and the company broadly; recruiting true believers. Never "done"; needs to be constant.

John Doerr: "we need teams of missionaries, not teams of mercenaries."

### The role of management — three critically important responsibilities

People managers (director of product management, director of UX design, managers/directors of engineering) must understand and communicate vision, strategy, principles and priorities, plus:

1. **Staffing** — sourcing, recruiting, interviewing, on-boarding, evaluating, replacing team members. HR supports but is in no way a substitute for the manager.
2. **Coaching** — the single most important yet most often overlooked element of capable management; at minimum a weekly 1:1. Develop skills; not micro-managing; "connecting the dots" across teams. Every member of a product team deserves someone committed to helping them get better at their craft — engineers report to engineering managers, designers to design managers, PMs to managers of product management.
3. **Objectives** — ensure each product team has a clear set of assigned objectives (typically quarterly) spelling out the problems they are asked to solve; most commonly OKRs. The team is given a small number of significant problems (objectives), proposes clear measures of success (key results). The litmus test for empowerment: the team is able to decide the best way to solve the problems they've been assigned.

## Ordinary people, and what to look for

Against "cultural fit" and "10X employees": results come from product teams; a toxic 10X employee does far more damage than good. What to consider instead:

1. **Competence** — capabilities, skills, track record (Stephen Covey: "trust is a function of two things: competence and character"). Table stakes. "A's hire A's, but B's hire C's" — a manager who is not accomplished herself is ill-equipped to assess candidates and coach them. Hiring for potential is OK if and only if the hiring manager actively coaches the person to competence.
2. **Character** — instead of "cultural fit" (which in practice means "hire people that look and think like we do"), keep the pool large and filter out the few assholes — the All Blacks' "No Assholes Rule" (Bob Sutton, The No Asshole Rule). One damaging consequence of hiring "like us": they think like us — what you need is people that think differently. Look for people clearly not like yourselves: different environments, education, work experience, life experience.

## Summary

Great teams are comprised of ordinary people that are empowered and inspired.

Three litmus questions for whether your team is truly empowered:
1. Are you staffed with competent people with character, skilled across the competencies you need (product manager, product designer, engineers)?
2. Are you assigned problems to solve, rather than given lists of features to build?
3. Are you accountable to deliver business results (outcomes) rather than shipping features (output)?
